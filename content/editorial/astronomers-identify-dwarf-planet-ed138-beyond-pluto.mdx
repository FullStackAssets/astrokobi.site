On October 28, 2026, a team of astronomers announced the detection of a new trans‑Neptunian object that may qualify as a dwarf planet. The object, provisionally labeled ED138, was identified in data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, which began full‑sky surveys in 2025. Its estimated diameter of 600–1,500 km would make it larger than the smallest recognized dwarf planet, Ceres. If confirmed, ED138 would join the handful of worlds officially classified by the IAU. The discovery reshapes our view of the solar system’s distant frontier.

<Callout type="takeaway">ED138 could become the sixth known dwarf planet, extending the solar system’s outer boundary.</Callout>

## What happened
Astronomers using the Rubin Observatory’s 8,800‑foot telescope ran a custom program to sift through billions of moving‑object detections from early 2026. The algorithm flagged ED138, prompting senior astrophysicist Matthew Holman and Rubin Solar System lead scientist Mario Jurić to verify the signal and rule out software glitches.

Further analysis uncovered 20 additional detections dating back to March 2026, and archival data allowed the team to trace the object's orbit for roughly a decade. ED138 now appears to orbit at about 46 astronomical units from the Sun, placing it well beyond Pluto, and its brightness suggests a size between 600 and 1,500 km.

## Why it matters
Adding a new dwarf planet would expand the catalog of known distant bodies, offering fresh clues about the formation and evolution of the outer solar system. The find also showcases the Rubin Observatory’s ability to discover faint, slow‑moving objects across the entire sky, reinforcing its role as a cornerstone for planetary science. However, the object's true nature remains uncertain until its mass and shape can be measured, a task that will require additional observations and possibly future space missions.

## How to think about it
Treat ED138 as a promising candidate rather than a confirmed member of the dwarf‑planet family. Track updates from the Minor Planet Center and upcoming measurement campaigns that aim to determine its mass and shape. For educators, use the discovery as a case study in how large‑scale surveys transform planetary science, emphasizing the iterative nature of classification.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume ED138 is officially a dwarf planet until its size and mass are precisely measured; premature claims can mislead public understanding.</Callout>
