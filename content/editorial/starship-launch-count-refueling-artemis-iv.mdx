Artemis IV will be NASA’s first crewed lunar landing that could rely on SpaceX’s Starship as the lander. The mission architecture calls for Starship to be refueled in high‑Earth orbit before heading to the Moon, but the exact number of launches required remains a moving target. Early estimates ranged from a GAO audit’s 16 launches to an advisory panel’s 22, while SpaceX’s Elon Musk has suggested a much lower figure of six to eight. This uncertainty matters because each additional launch adds cost, schedule risk, and operational complexity. Understanding the refueling plan is therefore as crucial as the spacecraft itself.

## What happened
A Government Accountability Office audit released in 2021 estimated that up to 16 Starship launches could be required to support an Artemis lunar landing. Later, a member of the Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel suggested the number could be as high as 22, reflecting concerns about the reliability of in‑space refueling. SpaceX’s founder Elon Musk publicly countered those figures, posting that the count would likely fall between six and eight launches based on Starship’s projected performance.

NASA’s recent Artemis update included a graphic outlining the refueling workflow for Artemis IV. After the Space Launch System lifts the Orion crew capsule into high Earth orbit, Orion will dock with a Starship lunar lander. At that point, Starship’s upper stage is expected to receive propellant from one or more dedicated tanker flights before the combined stack burns toward the Moon. The plan envisions the lander delivering two astronauts to the lunar surface, then re‑docking with Orion for the return trip.

## Why it matters
The launch count directly influences the program’s budget and timeline; each additional Starship flight incurs launch‑service costs, recovery operations, and refurbishment cycles. Moreover, the in‑orbit refueling steps have never been demonstrated at the scale required for a crewed lunar mission, making them a critical technical risk. Delays or failures in the tanker flights could push the Artemis IV landing window back, affecting NASA’s broader lunar exploration roadmap and commercial partners’ revenue expectations.

## How to think about it
Treat the launch‑count question as a range rather than a single figure. Evaluate each scenario (6‑8, ~16, ~22 launches) against cost, risk, and schedule metrics. Track the outcomes of Starship’s upcoming tanker test flights, as they will provide the first empirical data on propellant transfer reliability. Incorporate contingency buffers into budget and timeline plans to accommodate potential refueling setbacks. Finally, consider the broader strategic value: even a higher‑launch scenario could still be worthwhile if it establishes a reusable lunar‑transport capability for future Artemis missions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a single launch count or budget the mission on the lowest estimate without accounting for the high‑risk refueling operations.</Callout>
