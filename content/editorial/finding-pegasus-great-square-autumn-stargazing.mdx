Each autumn evening in the Northern Hemisphere, the constellation Pegasus climbs above the eastern horizon, announcing the season’s night sky. Its signature Great Square spans roughly 20 degrees, about two fists held at arm’s length, making it one of the easiest asterisms to spot. The square’s four corner stars—Algenib, Scheat, Markab, and Alpheratz—range from 2.1 to 2.8 magnitude and lie between 97 and 390 light‑years away. Beyond its utility for navigation, Pegasus carries a 3,000‑year‑old Greek myth that links the horse to heroic rescues, enriching the observing experience.

<Callout type="takeaway">Pegasus’s bright, spacious Great Square turns autumn stargazing into a simple, culturally rich guide.</Callout>

## What happened
Pegasus is the seventh‑largest of the 88 official constellations and dominates the autumn sky with its giant square asterism. The Great Square rises in the east at nightfall early in the season and climbs to near‑zenith by the end of autumn, covering about 20 degrees from top to bottom.

The four corner stars are Algenib (mag 2.8, 390 ly), Scheat (mag 2.4, 199 ly), Markab (mag 2.5, 140 ly) and Alpheratz (mag 2.1, 97 ly, technically part of Andromeda). Extending from Markab, the fainter stars Homam (mag 3.4) and Biham (mag 3.5) point toward the head star Enif (mag 2.4), which helps locate the globular cluster M15. Five degrees west of Scheat lies the 3rd‑magnitude star Matar, a useful marker for spotting nearby galaxies.

## Why it matters
The Great Square offers a reliable, low‑light navigation tool for amateur astronomers, allowing quick orientation and easy access to deeper‑sky targets such as M15 and surrounding galaxies. Its prominence also provides a tangible link between modern observation and the ancient Greek story of Pegasus, reinforcing how cultural narratives can enhance scientific engagement. Seasonal visibility means the constellation serves as a calendar marker, signaling the transition into autumn for skywatchers in the Northern Hemisphere and spring for those in the Southern Hemisphere.

## How to think about it
Start by locating the brightest corner, Alpheratz, then trace the square to Markab, Scheat, and Algenib. Use the square’s edges to gauge 20 degrees and estimate the position of Enif for deeper‑sky hunting. When planning a session, check local sunset times and aim for the first half of the night to catch Pegasus low on the eastern horizon, then follow its rise to a higher altitude for optimal viewing of associated clusters and galaxies.

<Callout type="warning">Avoid bright city lights when trying to trace the faint head stars; light pollution can hide Enif and the surrounding galaxies.</Callout>
