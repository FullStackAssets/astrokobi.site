On October 31, a telescope captured a striking view of the De Gerlache‑Kocher massif along the Moon’s southern limb. The image, featured in NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day on November 7, shows a rugged formation that towers roughly 4 km above the surrounding terrain. Its location near the lunar south pole places it at the intersection of permanently shadowed craters—potential reservoirs of water ice—and peaks that enjoy near‑continuous sunlight. These contrasting environments make the massif a focal point for planners of the next Artemis crewed missions. Understanding this terrain is key to turning science fiction about lunar bases into reality.
<Callout type="takeaway">The De Gerlache‑Kocher massif could become the first lunar outpost, thanks to its mix of shadowed ice traps and sun‑lit peaks.</Callout>
## What happened
The NASA‑released image was taken during a favorable libration, a slight wobble of the Moon that exposed the otherwise hidden southern limb. This geometry allowed a clear, oblique view of the massif, revealing its steep walls and the dark, permanently shadowed craters that rim its base.
Scientists note that the massif rises about 4 km above the surrounding plains, creating a dramatic contrast between sun‑lit ridges and deep shadows. The shadows are thought to preserve water‑ice deposits left by cometary impacts, while the sun‑lit peaks receive sunlight for most of the lunar day, a rare combination at high latitudes.
## Why it matters
Water ice can be harvested for life‑support consumables and propellant, reducing the need to launch these resources from Earth. Continuous sunlight, on the other hand, offers a reliable power source for habitats, rovers, and scientific equipment. Together, they lower the logistical burden of establishing a sustainable presence on the Moon.
For Artemis mission planners, the massif presents a natural “dual‑resource” site: a place where solar panels can be deployed on bright slopes while ice‑rich regolith can be mined in adjacent shadows. Securing such a location could accelerate the timeline for a permanent lunar base and serve as a testbed for technologies needed on Mars.

## How to think about it
When evaluating lunar sites, weigh resource availability against terrain difficulty. Map shadowed craters for ice potential, then overlay solar‑incidence models to locate sun‑lit zones. Prioritize locations where the two overlap within a short traverse distance, and factor in communication line‑of‑sight and launch‑site latitude. This framework helps decision‑makers balance scientific payoff with engineering risk.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that shadowed ice is easily extractable; extreme cold and regolith hardness require specialized mining hardware.
</Callout>
