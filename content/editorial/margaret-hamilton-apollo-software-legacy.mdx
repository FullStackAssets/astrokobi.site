Margaret Hamilton’s story begins with a childhood curiosity about gender roles and a love of the night sky. In the late 1950s she moved from meteorology to a MIT project that would design in‑flight computers for NASA. By 1965 she had become director of software engineering for the Apollo Guidance Computer, leading an all‑male team while insisting on rigorous error‑handling. During Apollo 11’s descent, an overload alarm threatened to abort the landing, but Hamilton’s code automatically dropped non‑essential tasks and kept the guidance system running, allowing Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to walk on the Moon.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hamilton’s foresight in building resilient software turned a potential disaster into a historic triumph.</Callout>

## What happened
Hamilton grew up in the 1940s and 1950s, confronting gender barriers by excelling in math and insisting on participation in any field she chose. After stints in meteorology and satellite tracking, she joined a MIT effort to create the Apollo Guidance Computer and quickly rose to direct its software division. Her team wrote the onboard code that monitored hundreds of variables and could re‑prioritize tasks when the computer’s cycle time was exceeded.

When the lunar module’s radar altimeter sent spurious data, the computer’s overload alarm lit up. Hamilton’s software contained a priority‑interrupt routine that discarded lower‑priority calculations, preserving navigation and attitude control. The system continued to guide the module to a safe touchdown, averting an abort.

Hamilton’s later years saw her advocate for software engineering as a discipline, co‑founding the company Hamilton Technologies and promoting the term “software engineering” to emphasize rigor and safety.

## Why it matters
The Apollo incident proved that anticipating failure is as important as achieving functionality, a lesson that underpins modern aerospace, automotive, and medical device software. Hamilton’s insistence on formal verification and documentation set standards that today’s regulators cite when certifying safety‑critical code. Moreover, her leadership as a woman in a male‑dominated field highlights the performance gains that diverse teams can deliver.

## How to think about it
Treat every critical system as a collection of prioritized tasks that can be shed gracefully under load. Build explicit interrupt routines that define which functions are essential for safety and which can be deferred. Encourage cross‑disciplinary teams and give space for unconventional problem‑solvers to challenge assumptions.
