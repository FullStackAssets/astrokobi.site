For decades astronomers have cataloged Trojan asteroids sharing Jupiter's orbit, and later those of Saturn and Uranus, but Neptune's co‑orbitals remained elusive. A team of Queen's University Belfast students, working with an international collaboration, has now confirmed two objects that trail Neptune, plus a third candidate, using data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. The find fills a missing piece in our map of the outer Solar System and opens new avenues for studying planetary migration.

<Callout type="takeaway">Neptune now has confirmed Trojan companions, ending a century‑long observational gap.</Callout>

## What happened
The researchers mined the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) data stream from the Rubin Observatory, which began full operations in June 2025 and repeatedly images the southern sky with an 8.4‑meter telescope and the world’s largest digital camera. By applying motion‑filter algorithms tuned to Neptune’s orbital speed, they isolated three faint, slow‑moving sources that share Neptune’s orbital period and reside near the planet’s L5 Lagrange point.

Two of those sources meet the dynamical criteria for permanent Trojans, while the third remains a candidate pending further astrometric confirmation. The discovery was reported in a paper submitted to The Astrophysical Journal Letters, listing Dr. Meg Schwamb as the lead author and acknowledging contributions from institutes across Europe, North America, and South America.

## Why it matters
Neptune’s Trojan population has been the missing link in comparative studies of planetary co‑orbitals, which inform models of early Solar System migration and the capture efficiency of giant planets. Confirming Trojans at Neptune validates predictions from the Nice model and related simulations that suggest all four gas giants should host such swarms. Moreover, the detection showcases Rubin’s capability to uncover faint, distant objects that were previously beyond reach, promising a richer inventory of outer‑Solar‑System bodies.

## How to think about it
Treat the discovery as a proof‑of‑concept that Rubin can populate the outer‑Solar‑System census. When evaluating planetary‑formation theories, incorporate Neptune’s Trojans as a boundary condition, but weight them lightly until a larger sample emerges. For amateur observers, monitor the reported coordinates during opposition windows to contribute supplemental astrometry.

<Callout type="warning">The current sample of two confirmed Trojans is too small to define the full population; avoid extrapolating size distributions from these alone.</Callout>
