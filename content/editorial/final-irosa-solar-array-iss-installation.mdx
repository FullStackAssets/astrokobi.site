On November 5, 2026, Expedition 75 flight engineers Anil Menon and Joshua Kutryk completed a six‑hour‑and‑59‑minute EVA to install the eighth and final International Space Station Roll‑Out Solar Array (iROSA). The new 63‑foot carbon‑composite array unfolded without motors, joining the station’s legacy solar wings. This addition can raise the ISS’s generated electricity by as much as 30 percent, extending its operational lifespan. The successful deployment marks the culmination of a 15‑year upgrade program that began with the first iROSA in 2021.

<Callout type="takeaway">The final iROSA installation gives the ISS a significant power boost and completes a decade‑long solar upgrade.</Callout>

## What happened
Menon and Kutryk began the EVA at 8:36 a.m. EST, retrieving the 750‑pound (340 kg) iROSA from a temporary stowage platform using Canadarm2 and mounting it on the starboard side of the 3B power channel. After securing the array to its bracket kit, they routed power cables to integrate it with the station’s electrical system.

During the hookup, Mission Control reported only half of the expected shunt current, prompting a pause while the crew waited for the station to enter Earth’s shadow to safely inspect the connectors. Once the issue was cleared, the team received a go‑for‑deploy signal.

With the bolts released, the array unfurled to its full 63‑foot length in about ten minutes, relying solely on the stored potential energy of its rolled‑up carbon‑composite booms. The EVA concluded at 3:35 p.m. EST, marking the completion of all eight iROSA installations.

## Why it matters
The additional iROSA increases the ISS’s power generation capacity by up to 30 percent, providing a larger margin for scientific payloads, life‑support systems, and future commercial modules. Higher power availability also reduces reliance on older, less efficient solar wings, extending the station’s service life as NASA plans for its eventual transition to private‑sector operation. Moreover, the roll‑out design demonstrates a low‑mass, low‑complexity solar technology that could be adapted for lunar or Martian habitats.

## How to think about it
When evaluating power upgrades for orbital platforms, weigh the incremental wattage against EVA risk, integration time, and long‑term maintenance. The iROSA rollout shows that passive deployment can reduce mechanical failure points, but thorough ground testing of electrical interfaces remains essential. For future habitats, consider modular solar panels that can be added without extensive EVA work, leveraging robotic arms for installation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt to service or modify high‑voltage solar arrays without proper EVA training and real‑time ground support, as unexpected shunt currents can pose serious safety hazards.</Callout>
