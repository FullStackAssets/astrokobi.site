When astronomers measured the quasar J0313-1806 at less than 700 million years after the Big Bang, they found a black hole already weighing about three billion suns.  Standard Eddington‑limited growth predicts a mass‑doubling factor of e every 45 million years, meaning a stellar‑mass seed would need roughly 770 million years—more time than the universe had.  Two conventional shortcuts—starting from a massive “heavy seed” of ten‑thousand solar masses or sustaining super‑Eddington accretion—each rely on rare conditions.  A new study proposes a third, older idea: chaotic accretion, which can accelerate growth without extreme seeds or broken speed limits.

<Callout type="takeaway">Chaotic accretion can accelerate black‑hole growth without invoking massive seeds or sustained super‑Eddington rates.</Callout>

## What happened
The quasar J0313-1806, observed at a cosmic age under 700 million years, hosts a black hole of roughly three billion solar masses.  At the canonical Eddington limit, where radiation pressure balances gravity, a black hole gains mass by a factor of e every 45 million years if about ten percent of the infalling gas is radiated away.  A hundred‑solar‑mass stellar seed would therefore need about seventeen such e‑foldings—around 770 million years—to reach the observed mass, leaving insufficient time after accounting for the seed’s formation.

Researchers have traditionally invoked two ways out.  The first posits a “heavy seed” formed by direct collapse of a massive gas cloud, requiring at least ten‑thousand solar masses to skip the slowest growth phase.  The second assumes accretion can stay super‑Eddington for hundreds of millions of years, but both scenarios face uncertainties about how common the required conditions are.

In a recent paper, the authors revisit chaotic accretion, where gas streams arrive with random orientations, repeatedly torquing and spinning down the black hole.  Spin‑down reduces the radiative efficiency, meaning a larger fraction of the inflowing mass adds to the black hole’s weight, effectively boosting growth while still respecting the Eddington luminosity limit.

## Why it matters
If chaotic accretion proved common, it would reshape models of early galaxy formation, easing the tension between observed massive quasars and theoretical growth timelines.  It also lessens the need for exotic seed formation pathways, allowing standard stellar remnants to seed the first supermassive black holes.  Observationally, signatures such as variable jet orientations or irregular emission lines could become diagnostics for chaotic feeding, guiding future surveys with JWST and next‑generation X‑ray observatories.

## How to think about it
When evaluating early‑universe black‑hole growth, treat chaotic accretion as a third axis alongside seed mass and accretion rate.  First, estimate the likely spin state of a nascent black hole; a lower spin means higher radiative efficiency and slower growth.  Second, assess the environment’s propensity for misaligned gas streams—mergers, filamentary inflows, and turbulent halos all promote chaos.  Finally, incorporate spin‑evolution equations into semi‑analytic models to see how quickly a black hole can climb the mass ladder under realistic chaotic conditions.

<Callout type="warning">The chaotic accretion scenario is still theoretical; avoid assuming it solves all early‑black‑hole puzzles without further observational confirmation.</Callout>
