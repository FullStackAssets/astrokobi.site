Astrophysicists have just tightened the net around one of the most exotic dark‑matter ideas. A new study using the Planck satellite’s map of the cosmic microwave background shows that stupendously large black holes—objects with masses up to 10 billion billion suns—cannot make up the missing mass in the Universe. The analysis looks for tiny temperature fluctuations that such massive objects would imprint on the early light. By finding none, the researchers rule out this class of primordial black holes as a viable dark‑matter candidate. This result reshapes the landscape of theories still hunting for the elusive substance.

<Callout type="takeaway">Stupendously large black holes are now excluded as the primary component of dark matter.</Callout>

## What happened

The team published a paper in *Physical Review Letters* titled “Closing the Mass Window for Stupendously Large Black Holes.” Using the high‑precision anisotropy measurements from ESA’s Planck mission, they modeled how black holes with masses from 100 million to 10 billion billion times that of the Sun would disturb the CMB’s temperature pattern. Their calculations show that any significant population of such objects would produce detectable distortions, which are absent in the data.

From this null result they derived an upper limit on the fraction of dark matter that could be composed of SLABs, finding it to be effectively zero. The conclusion is that SLABs cannot account for the dark‑matter density inferred from galaxy rotation curves and large‑scale structure.

## Why it matters

Eliminating SLABs sharpens the focus of dark‑matter research, removing one of the most massive primordial‑black‑hole scenarios from consideration. It also validates the power of CMB observations as a probe of exotic compact objects, complementing gravitational‑wave and microlensing searches. The finding pushes theorists toward lighter black‑hole mass windows or entirely different particle candidates, and it informs future survey designs that aim to test the remaining possibilities.

## How to think about it

Treat dark‑matter investigations as a process of systematic elimination: combine independent constraints—from the CMB, gravitational waves, galaxy surveys, and laboratory experiments—to carve out the remaining parameter space. When a candidate is ruled out, reassess the theoretical motivations and explore adjacent mass ranges or interaction models. Maintaining a flexible, evidence‑first mindset helps avoid over‑committing to any single hypothesis.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume all primordial black holes are excluded; only the stupendously massive end of the spectrum has been ruled out by this study.</Callout>
