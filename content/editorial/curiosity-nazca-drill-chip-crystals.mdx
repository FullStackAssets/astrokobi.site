During sols 5063‑5069, NASA’s Curiosity rover intensified its drill campaign at the Nazca site, targeting a bedrock region dotted with unusual disk‑shaped crystal formations. The rover’s drill extracted material that was sent to the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument while ChemCam, Mastcam and MAHLI captured detailed spectroscopic and imaging data of several crystal targets. These coordinated observations aim to decipher the mineralogy and formation history of the chip‑like structures, a key step toward understanding Mars’ past aqueous environments.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Nazca drill campaign provides the first in‑situ compositional data on Mars’ chip‑shaped crystals.</Callout>

## What happened
On Sol 5063 the rover completed drilling into the Nazca block, retrieving core material that was transferred to SAM for gas‑chromatography and mass‑spectrometry analysis. Simultaneously, ChemCam performed LIBS scans of the newly exposed “Ahi Veremos” crystal formation, while Mastcam added frames to a growing mosaic of the surrounding terrain.

The following sols expanded the campaign: Sol 5065 featured LIBS depth profiles of the “Colca” crystals and a LIBS measurement of the “Bermejo” target; Sol 5066 captured a long‑distance Remote Micro‑Imager (RMI) mosaic of rock clusters beneath the Cordillera ridge. On Sol 5068 the rover used MAHLI’s nighttime white‑light and UV LEDs to image the crystal surfaces, and also recorded Phobos eclipsing the Sun. A final drive on Sol 5069 repositioned Curiosity toward its southern traverse while continuing environmental monitoring, including dust‑devil surveys.

## Why it matters
Understanding the composition of these chip‑shaped crystals could reveal whether liquid water once percolated through the Nazca formation, informing models of ancient habitability. SAM’s chemical fingerprints, combined with ChemCam’s elemental maps, allow scientists to test hypotheses about mineral precipitation in evaporitic or hydrothermal settings. The data also refine landing‑site selection criteria for future missions that aim to retrieve samples or search for biosignatures.

## How to think about it
Treat the Nazca findings as a case study in layered planetary investigation: first identify a visually distinct target, then acquire compositional data (SAM), map elemental distribution (ChemCam), and finally contextualize with high‑resolution imaging (Mastcam, MAHLI, RMI). When evaluating future sites, ask whether the target exhibits unique morphology, whether the rover can safely drill, and whether the suite of instruments can cross‑validate the results.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate the chemistry of a single drill core to the entire Nazca formation without additional sampling.</Callout>
