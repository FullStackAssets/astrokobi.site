NASA announced Wednesday that it has selected three companies to develop Ka‑band satellite relay concepts that can work with existing spacecraft without hardware changes. The firms—Astranis Space Technologies, Global Enterprise Solutions, and a third partner—will receive firm‑fixed‑price contracts worth up to $264.3 million, $340.4 million, and a comparable amount, with an initial performance window from Nov 4 2026 to May 3 2027 and options extending to 2029. The effort is part of NASA’s SCaN program and the NEXT‑3 Broad Agency Announcement, aiming to replace the aging Tracking and Data Relay Satellite (TDRS) system with commercial Ka‑band services. If successful, the contracts could secure continuous communications for missions that cannot be retrofitted, while opening a market for private relay providers.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s Ka‑band contracts mark a decisive shift from government‑run TDRS to commercial relay services for future missions.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA selected three industry partners to design end‑to‑end Ka‑band relay solutions that include space hardware, ground stations, launch integration, and operational support. The awards are firm‑fixed‑price under the Next Space Technologies for Exploration Partnerships‑3 (NEXT‑3) program, with maximum potential values of $264.3 million for Astranis and $340.4 million for Global Enterprise, plus a similar ceiling for the third awardee. The initial contract period runs from 4 Nov 2026 through 3 May 2027, with priced options that could extend development and a flight‑demonstration phase through 2029.

The contracts support NASA’s broader strategy to gradually retire the TDRS fleet and transition to commercially provided Ka‑band services. By targeting missions that cannot modify onboard systems, the work aims to preserve data continuity while reducing reliance on aging government satellites.

## Why it matters
Replacing TDRS with commercial Ka‑band relays could lower long‑term operating costs and stimulate a new market for high‑frequency space communications. Continuous connectivity is essential for scientific, exploration, and Earth‑observation missions, especially those in low‑Earth orbit that currently depend on TDRS. A successful commercial solution also diversifies the communications ecosystem, reducing single‑point‑failure risk. However, the transition introduces uncertainty about reliability and timing, as private providers must demonstrate performance comparable to the proven TDRS network.

## How to think about it
Assess each mission’s communication requirements and determine whether a hybrid approach—using both legacy TDRS and emerging Ka‑band services—offers the safest path during the transition. Track milestone progress of the three contracts, especially any in‑orbit demonstration results, before committing new missions solely to commercial relays. Incorporate risk‑mitigation plans that include fallback to TDRS or alternative ground‑station networks until commercial services prove reliable.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume commercial Ka‑band services will be available for all missions immediately; legacy TDRS capacity will remain essential during the transition.</Callout>
