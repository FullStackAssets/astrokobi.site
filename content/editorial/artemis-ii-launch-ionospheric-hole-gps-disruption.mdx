The Artemis II mission lifted off in April 2026, sending the most powerful SLS rocket into space. The launch generated a massive plume that punched a temporary hole in the ionosphere, the charged layer above 30 miles. Ground‑based GPS receivers recorded electron density changes that translated into positioning errors of several meters. Those errors are enough to misguide self‑driving cars and drones, highlighting a new vulnerability of modern navigation systems.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Artemis II launch produced the strongest ionospheric disturbance ever recorded, briefly degrading GPS accuracy by meters.</Callout>

## What happened
Researchers from National Cheng Kung University examined high‑precision GPS networks that monitor tectonic motion. By measuring signal travel times they reconstructed how the SLS plume altered free‑electron concentrations in the ionosphere. The resulting electron density shift produced positioning errors of several meters at ground stations.

The disturbance was measured to be more significant than any previous rocket launch and up to ten times more intense than the ionospheric effects observed after SpaceX’s Starship test flights, despite Starship’s higher thrust on paper.

Artemis II marked the first crewed lunar mission in over fifty years, a step toward a sustained human presence on the Moon, but its launch also revealed an unexpected side effect on Earth’s upper atmosphere.

## Why it matters
Modern transportation increasingly relies on centimeter‑level GPS data for autonomous cars, delivery drones, and precision agriculture. A few‑meter error can cause lane‑change miscalculations, route deviations, or loss of control in tightly regulated airspace. Beyond safety, the ionospheric anomaly challenges scientific measurements that depend on stable GPS baselines, such as earthquake‑early‑warning systems.

## How to think about it
Treat space‑launch‑induced ionospheric disturbances as a weather‑like factor in navigation planning. Integrate launch schedules into GPS‑error forecasting tools, and design autonomous systems to cross‑check positioning with inertial sensors or local beacons when large launches are imminent. Policy makers should consider guidelines for notifying critical‑infrastructure operators ahead of high‑energy launches.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every rocket launch will cause similar GPS outages; the effect depends on launch trajectory, thrust profile, and atmospheric conditions. Avoid disabling GPS entirely—use complementary positioning methods instead.</Callout>
