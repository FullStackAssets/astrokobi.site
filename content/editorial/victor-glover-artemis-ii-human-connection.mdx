Victor Glover, one of the four astronauts aboard NASA's Artemis II mission, reflected on his lunar flyby during a conversation at the Starmus VIII festival in Tenerife. The crew launched on April 1, 2026, traveling over 250 million miles and becoming the first humans to journey beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. As Orion passed behind the Moon, Earth vanished from view, and radio contact was lost for about 40 minutes before the planet reappeared. Glover said the experience taught him that the greatest reason to go to space is not heroics but a renewed appreciation for human connection, a feeling shared by only 651 people who have ever left Earth’s surface.

<Callout type="takeaway">Spaceflight’s deepest reward is the perspective that strengthens our sense of shared humanity.</Callout>

## What happened
Artemis II lifted off on April 1, 2026, carrying Victor Glover, Reid Wiseman, Christina Koch, and Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen. The mission marked humanity’s first crewed lunar venture in more than five decades. While traversing the far side of the Moon, the crew watched Earth dip below the lunar horizon, causing a brief loss of radio contact that lasted roughly 40 minutes before the planet re‑emerged.

During a post‑flight discussion, Glover emphasized that the profound moment was not the view itself but the feeling of missing Earth and the billions of people who inhabit it. He noted that if anyone could experience that distance, they would likely return with a similar yearning for connection.

## Why it matters
Glover’s insight reframes the narrative around space exploration from a pursuit of technical milestones to a catalyst for global empathy. By highlighting the emotional impact of seeing Earth as a fragile, shared home, the perspective can inspire broader public support for future missions and encourage policymakers to prioritize collaborative, human‑focused goals. The message also resonates beyond aerospace, reminding societies that shared challenges—climate change, pandemics, inequality—benefit from a unified outlook.

## How to think about it
Treat the “overview effect” as a lens rather than a guarantee. Integrate astronauts’ reflections into education curricula, public outreach, and policy briefs to humanize the abstract goals of spaceflight. Pair the emotional narrative with concrete data on how space technologies benefit everyday life—weather forecasting, communications, and environmental monitoring—to build a balanced case for continued investment.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every person will experience the same profound connection; the effect varies with individual background and cultural context.</Callout>
