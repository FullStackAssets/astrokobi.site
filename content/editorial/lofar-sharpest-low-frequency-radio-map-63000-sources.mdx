A team led by Leiden University has used the Low Frequency Array (LOFAR) to produce the sharpest and deepest low‑frequency radio image ever recorded. The new map covers the ELAIS‑N1 field, a sky patch about 25 times the area of the full Moon, and reveals almost 63,000 distinct radio sources. With a resolution fine enough to pick out a coin from ten kilometres away, the image contains eight billion pixels and required 200 hours of observation and 400 TB of raw data. The achievement is possible thanks to new calibration and data‑reduction pipelines that cut processing costs by more than a factor of ten. Making the data publicly available opens the door for multi‑wavelength studies that can probe galaxy evolution across cosmic time.

<Callout type="takeaway">LOFAR’s new map delivers unprecedented low‑frequency detail, unlocking a massive catalog of faint radio sources.</Callout>

## What happened
The international LOFAR team pointed the array at the well‑studied ELAIS‑N1 region, integrating 200 hours of low‑frequency observations that produced an 8‑billion‑pixel image. This image is roughly 20 times sharper than the previous deepest LOFAR view of the same field, achieving a resolution comparable to spotting a coin from ten kilometres away.

Processing the 400 TB data set was made feasible by the Spider high‑performance computing platform at SURF, which reduced the reduction time to a few months. Advances in calibration and data‑reduction methods lowered the computational cost by more than an order of magnitude, enabling the creation of the deepest low‑frequency sky map to date.

The final product, now publicly released on the LOFAR archive, lists nearly 63,000 radio sources—including active galactic nuclei and star‑forming galaxies—providing a foundation for cross‑matching with optical, infrared, and X‑ray surveys.

## Why it matters
By pushing low‑frequency radio imaging to unprecedented depth and clarity, the map fills a critical gap in our view of the universe, where many distant, faint galaxies emit most of their power. The massive source catalog will sharpen statistical studies of galaxy evolution, dark matter distribution, and cosmic magnetism. Moreover, open access to the data encourages collaborative, multi‑wavelength research, accelerating discoveries that rely on combining radio information with other parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

## How to think about it
Researchers should treat the LOFAR map as a baseline layer to be overlaid with higher‑frequency or higher‑resolution surveys. Start by cross‑matching the catalog with optical/IR databases to assign redshifts, then use machine‑learning classifiers to separate AGN from star‑forming galaxies. Plan for adequate storage and consider using cloud‑based HPC services if local infrastructure cannot handle the 400 TB dataset. Finally, contribute back any derived products—such as refined source classifications—to the community archive.
