JWST’s unprecedented view of the distant universe still leaves nearby planetary systems shrouded in stellar glare. A Northwestern University team has built a computational pipeline that models nanometer‑scale telescope imperfections and uses machine learning to subtract that glare. Applying the pipeline to two‑year‑old JWST observations uncovered a giant exoplanet that vanished under conventional processing. The find shows that existing archives may already contain dozens of hidden worlds, extending JWST’s scientific return without new hardware.

<Callout type="takeaway">Advanced glare‑removal can turn old JWST images into a treasure trove of hidden exoplanets.</Callout>

## What happened
Stars outshine their planets by factors of a million or more, making direct imaging extremely challenging. The new method combines physics‑based ray‑tracing of the telescope’s optics with a machine‑learning model trained on known instrumental variations, allowing it to predict and subtract the diffraction pattern caused by minute shifts and tilts of the mirrors.

When the researchers re‑processed archival JWST data from the past two years, the algorithm boosted the effective sensitivity by up to fifteen times. In the cleaned image a giant exoplanet—previously invisible—appeared as a faint point source orbiting its host star. The results were reported in a Nature paper published on November 4, 2026.

## Why it matters
By extracting planets that were hidden in existing data, the technique expands the census of exoplanets without requiring additional telescope time, lowering the cost of discovery. It also pushes the detection threshold toward smaller, colder worlds, sharpening our view of planetary system architectures and informing models of planet formation. Astronomers, mission planners, and the broader scientific community stand to benefit from a richer, more complete inventory of nearby exoplanets.

## How to think about it
Treat glare‑removed detections as candidates that require validation. Start by re‑examining the original raw frames to ensure the signal persists across processing steps. Cross‑match the candidate with other wavelengths or instruments, and where possible schedule follow‑up observations (e.g., radial‑velocity or transit monitoring) to confirm its planetary nature. Incorporate the method into a broader toolkit that includes coronagraphy and interferometry for a multi‑pronged search strategy.

<Callout type="warning">Do not accept a glare‑removed detection as definitive without follow‑up observations or independent data sets.</Callout>
