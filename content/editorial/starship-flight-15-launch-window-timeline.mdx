SpaceX is gearing up for its most ambitious Starship test yet: Flight 15, the first attempt to recover both the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage at the same launch site. The mission is slated for Friday, Nov 13, with a launch window that starts no earlier than 5:45 p.m. EST (2245 GMT) and can extend to 7 p.m. EST, giving the team a 75‑minute flexibility buffer. If everything proceeds as planned, the two stages will touch down back at Starbase on Saturday, Nov 14, at 4:17 p.m. EST, making the flight just over 22.5 hours long. Success would mark the first full orbital rocket recovery in history and could accelerate Starship’s role in lunar and Martian missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Starship Flight 15 aims to achieve the first full orbital rocket recovery by landing both stages back at Starbase.</Callout>

## What happened
SpaceX announced that Flight 15 will launch from the Starbase site in South Texas on Nov 13, with a launch window of 5:45 p.m. to 7 p.m. EST (2245‑0000 GMT). The local Texas window translates to 4:45 p.m.‑6 p.m. CST. The mission will test a “double‑catch” sequence: the Super Heavy booster will perform a controlled return to the launch pad, followed seconds later by the Starship upper stage executing its own landing burn.

If the launch occurs at the end of the window, the timeline pushes the touchdown to later on Nov 14, but the overall mission duration remains roughly 22.5 hours. The flight is the 15th Starship test, following 14 prior attempts that have yielded nine successes and five failures as of late September 2026.

## Why it matters
A successful full recovery would prove that a two‑stage, fully reusable orbital vehicle can be turned around quickly and cheaply, a cornerstone of SpaceX’s strategy to lower launch costs and support NASA’s Artemis lunar program. Demonstrating rapid, on‑site refurbishment could also open the door for large‑scale satellite constellations, deep‑space habitats, and eventually crewed missions to Mars. Conversely, a failure would highlight the engineering challenges that still separate Starship from operational status, potentially delaying contracts and investor confidence.

## How to think about it
Treat the Flight 15 window as a probabilistic event rather than a fixed timestamp—track real‑time updates from SpaceX’s webcast and be ready for a shift up to 7 p.m. EST. For investors, weigh the outcome against SpaceX’s broader launch cadence and its contractual milestones with NASA. For enthusiasts, note that the double‑landing test is a prerequisite for future crewed lunar landings, so its success or failure will ripple through Artemis planning.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the launch will occur exactly at 5:45 p.m. EST; the 75‑minute window means the liftoff could be delayed, and weather or technical issues may push the mission to a backup day.
