At a conference in Munich, Isar Aerospace CEO Daniel Metzler announced that the German launch provider is already 15 times oversubscribed for its 2028 flight schedule. The company, which became the first privately financed European firm to place a rocket in orbit in September, now sees a backlog of 14 customers per rocket. Metzler said the shift from commercial to military demand and the development of reusable stages are designed to challenge SpaceX’s market dominance. If Isar can translate that demand into reliable, low‑cost launches, Europe’s satellite sector could gain a home‑grown alternative to the $1.9 trillion‑valued SpaceX.

<Callout type="takeaway">Isar’s oversubscribed 2028 launch pipeline could force SpaceX to defend its global lead.</Callout>

## What happened
Isar Aerospace reported that its 2028 launch manifest is 15 times oversubscribed, with roughly 14 customers waiting for each available slot. The demand mix has flipped from 85 % commercial and 15 % military to a majority of defense contracts, reflecting European governments’ preference for indigenous launch services.

The company is actively developing reusable rockets, planning to capture re‑entry data on the next launch’s first stage and to introduce hardware specifically designed for reusability over the next three to four flights. These efforts aim to lower per‑kilogram costs and bring European launch prices closer to those of SpaceX’s Falcon 9.

European policymakers view the rivalry between Isar and other newcomers such as Rocket Factory Augsburg as a lever to drive down launch prices, ensuring affordable access for telecommunications, Earth observation, and defense satellites.

## Why it matters
If Isar delivers on its backlog, Europe could secure a strategic launch capability independent of a single U.S. provider, reducing geopolitical risk for defense payloads. Competitive pricing would also broaden market participation for smaller satellite operators, potentially accelerating the continent’s broadband and climate‑monitoring initiatives. Conversely, failure to achieve reliable reusability could stall the momentum and leave European customers vulnerable to price spikes or capacity shortages.

## How to think about it
When assessing Isar’s trajectory, weigh three factors: (1) reliability track record – two successful orbital flights versus SpaceX’s four attempts, (2) maturity of reusable hardware – measured by successful re‑entry and refurbishment cycles, and (3) geopolitical alignment – the extent to which European defense contracts lock in demand. A balanced view recognizes both the commercial upside of a filled launch manifest and the engineering challenges inherent in scaling reusability.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that Isar’s current backlog guarantees on‑time delivery; capacity constraints and technology maturation could cause schedule slips.</Callout>
