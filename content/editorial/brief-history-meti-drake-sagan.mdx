When Frank Drake and Carl Sagan first crossed paths in 1961, they sparked a partnership that would give the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence a cultural and scientific foothold. Their informal symposium at Green Bank set the stage for the Drake Equation, a framework that still guides estimates of communicable civilizations. NASA soon took notice, commissioning the Pioneer Plaques and the Voyager Golden Records—physical messages now drifting through interstellar space. These artifacts embody the ambition to speak to unknown neighbors and illustrate how scientific curiosity can become a planetary legacy. Understanding this history clarifies why modern METI debates hinge on both technical feasibility and symbolic resonance.

<Callout type="takeaway">Drake and Sagan turned speculative ideas into lasting interstellar messages that still travel beyond our Solar System.</Callout>

## What happened
In early 1961, a year after Project Ozma, Drake organized an informal gathering at the Green Bank Observatory, inviting leading astronomers including a young Carl Sagan. The symposium crystallized the challenges of detecting alien signals and prompted Drake to formalize his famous equation, which enumerates factors such as star formation rate, planetary habitability, and civilization longevity.

The enthusiasm generated by the meeting attracted NASA’s interest, leading to the commissioning of two historic outreach projects. The Pioneer 10 and 11 spacecraft carried gold‑anodized aluminum plaques etched with a pictorial map of the Solar System and a human silhouette, while the later Voyager 1 and 2 missions included phonograph‑style Golden Records containing music, greetings, and images.

Both the plaques and the records are now traveling beyond the heliosphere, serving as humanity’s first physical postcards to the cosmos.

## Why it matters
These initiatives proved that messaging could be more than a radio transmission; it could be a tangible cultural artifact that endures across cosmic timescales. The plaques and records have become reference points for contemporary METI proposals, illustrating both the power of narrative to inspire public support and the technical constraints of encoding information for unknown recipients. Moreover, they set a precedent for international collaboration, as NASA partnered with scientists, artists, and linguists to craft a message that represents humanity.

## How to think about it
When evaluating new METI projects, weigh symbolic impact against scientific return. Start by asking whether the message advances a testable hypothesis or primarily serves as cultural outreach. Pair any transmission with transparent cost accounting and seek interdisciplinary review to ensure the content is both accurate and representative of humanity.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the Pioneer Plaques or Voyager records will be discovered; detection depends on many unknown factors.</Callout>
