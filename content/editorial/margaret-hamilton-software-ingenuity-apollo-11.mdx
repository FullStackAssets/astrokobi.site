When the lunar module’s computer overloaded during Apollo 11, the world held its breath. The crisis was averted by Margaret Hamilton’s fault‑tolerant software, which re‑prioritized essential tasks in seconds. Hamilton, the director of software engineering at MIT’s Instrumentation Laboratory, had spent a decade building that capability. Her work not only secured the first Moon landing but also set a template for resilient systems used today.

<Callout type="takeaway">Robust, priority‑based software design can turn a near‑disaster into a historic success.</Callout>

## What happened
Margaret Hamilton joined the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory in the mid‑1960s, eventually leading the team that wrote the onboard flight software for NASA’s Apollo missions. She introduced the concept of asynchronous, priority‑based task scheduling, allowing the computer to continue critical functions while less essential processes were temporarily suspended.

During the Apollo 11 descent, the guidance computer generated a “1202” alarm as it became overloaded with data from the rendezvous radar. Hamilton’s software automatically filtered out non‑essential tasks, keeping the navigation and control loops active and enabling Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to land safely.

Hamilton passed away on September 30 at age 90, leaving a legacy that extends far beyond the Moon landing, influencing modern aerospace, automotive, and even consumer‑device software architectures.

## Why it matters
The incident proved that software can be engineered to handle real‑time emergencies without human intervention, a principle that underpins today’s autonomous vehicles and spacecraft. Hamilton’s design philosophy—prioritize safety‑critical functions and degrade gracefully—remains a cornerstone of safety‑critical system standards. Moreover, her visibility as a female leader in a male‑dominated field continues to inspire broader participation in STEM.

## How to think about it
Treat software as a layered safety system: identify core mission‑critical functions, design them to run independently of lower‑priority code, and verify behavior under simulated overloads. Use watchdog timers and explicit priority queues to ensure graceful degradation. Regularly audit the codebase to distribute expertise and avoid single‑point knowledge failures.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on a single software fix to replace comprehensive system engineering and testing.</Callout>
