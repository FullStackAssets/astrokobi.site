Starship's Flight 15, slated for launch on November 13 from Starbase in South Texas, will attempt the first simultaneous recovery of both the Super Heavy booster and the Ship upper stage. The 75‑minute launch window opens at 5:45 p.m. EST, with a planned catch of the next‑gen Super Heavy “V3” about 6.5 minutes after liftoff. About 35 minutes into the flight, the vehicle is set to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites before the upper stage re‑enters over sparsely populated regions of northern Mexico and southern Texas, aiming for a belly‑flop maneuver and tower capture roughly 21 hours later. Success would mark the first full recovery of an orbital launch system and a major stride toward SpaceX’s goal of hourly launches by 2030.

<Callout type="takeaway">If successful, Flight 15 would deliver the first fully recovered orbital launch system.</Callout>

## What happened
SpaceX announced a 75‑minute window on Nov 13, targeting a 5:45 p.m. EST liftoff. The mission includes the first attempt to catch a Super Heavy “V3” booster using the tower’s mechanical arms, a maneuver expected around 6.5 minutes after launch. Following booster separation, the Ship upper stage will insert 26 Starlink V3 satellites into low‑Earth orbit, then continue on a trajectory that brings it over northern Mexico and southern Texas for a controlled re‑entry and tower capture about 21 hours later.

Since April 2023, Super Heavy has flown 14 times, achieving nine successes and five failures. To date, three boosters have been caught and two have been reflown, demonstrating a growing but still limited heritage for full‑stage recovery. The upcoming double‑catch test builds on this record, aiming to close the loop on reusability for both stages in a single mission.

## Why it matters
A successful double‑catch would validate the technical concept of rapid, turn‑key reuse for both launch stages, dramatically lowering per‑launch costs and enabling the high launch cadence SpaceX envisions for lunar, Martian, and Earth‑orbit services. Full stage recovery also reduces manufacturing demand, supporting the economies of scale needed for a sustainable interplanetary transport system. Conversely, a failure could underscore the remaining engineering and regulatory challenges, potentially slowing the timeline for hourly launches and Mars colonization plans.

## How to think about it
Treat Flight 15 as a milestone test rather than a production‑ready event. Evaluate the outcome on three axes: hardware performance (booster catch precision, upper‑stage heat‑shield assessment), operational cadence (turn‑around time between flights), and regulatory response (FAA approvals for repeated tower catches). Use these metrics to gauge how quickly SpaceX can move from experimental to routine double‑catch operations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a successful Flight 15 guarantees immediate hourly launches; the infrastructure, certification, and supply chain upgrades required will take additional years.</Callout>
