The European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope has just released its first major data set, dubbed DR1‑Foundation. After 13 months of observations, the release covers 1900 square degrees—about 14 % of the sky Euclid will eventually survey—and contains measurements for more than one billion objects. This trove includes visible‑light images and near‑infrared spectra that together begin to trace the cosmic web of galaxies, filaments, and voids. By expanding from the earlier 60‑square‑degree test patch to a region the size of roughly 9,500 full moons, Euclid is moving from proof‑of‑concept to a cornerstone of dark‑energy research.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid’s DR1‑Foundation provides the first billion‑object catalog that will underpin the mission’s ultimate 3D map of the universe.</Callout>

## What happened
Euclid’s DR1‑Foundation combines data from the Wide and Deep Surveys, the two pillars of the mission’s six‑year plan. The Wide Survey, an imaging and spectroscopic campaign in visible and near‑infrared, now spans 1900 square degrees, while the Deep Survey targets smaller, fainter regions within that footprint to capture dwarf galaxies and high‑redshift quasars. Together they deliver photometry and spectra for over a billion sources, a dramatic increase from the initial 60 square‑degree preview that featured a handful of well‑known objects such as the Horsehead Nebula and the Perseus cluster.

Compared with ground‑based efforts like the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), Euclid enjoys a space‑based platform free of atmospheric distortion, yielding higher resolution and deeper infrared coverage. Although DESI currently holds the record for the largest 3D map, Euclid’s eventual sky coverage and depth will surpass it, offering a more complete view of large‑scale structure and the influence of dark energy and dark matter across cosmic time.

## Why it matters
Mapping the distribution of matter on the largest scales is essential for testing theories of dark energy and dark matter. Euclid’s billion‑object catalog provides the statistical power needed to detect subtle signatures in the growth of cosmic structures, helping to discriminate between competing cosmological models. Researchers worldwide will be able to cross‑match Euclid’s data with other surveys, improving distance measurements and refining constraints on the Universe’s expansion history. The release also demonstrates the mission’s operational readiness, building confidence that the full six‑year survey will deliver the promised transformative insights.

## How to think about it
Treat DR1‑Foundation as a foundational layer rather than a final product. Use it to explore large‑scale structure, test pipelines, and develop cross‑survey synergies, but keep in mind that systematic uncertainties will be refined as more sky is added. When evaluating scientific claims, consider the current sky fraction and the depth of the data, and anticipate that future releases will expand coverage and improve precision.

<Callout type="warning">Do not overinterpret subtle statistical trends in the DR1 data; many systematics are still being quantified as the mission progresses.</Callout>
