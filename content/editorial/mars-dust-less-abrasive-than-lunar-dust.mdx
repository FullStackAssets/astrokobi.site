NASA's Perseverance rover has been carrying tiny squares of spacesuit material—each under a square inch (about 6.5 cm²)—since its landing. The swatches sit on the rover’s deck, directly exposed to the relentless Martian dust that whirls across the planet’s surface. After 1,645 Martian days, scientists examined the samples and found only faint, if any, signs of wear. By contrast, lunar dust from the Apollo era famously scratched visor windows and degraded suit fabrics. The new data suggest that Martian dust may be far less abrasive than its lunar counterpart.

<Callout type="takeaway">Martian dust is smoother and less likely to abrade spacesuits than the sharp lunar regolith.</Callout>

## What happened
The Perseverance rover’s deck holds several swatches of the same fabric used in modern EVA suits, each less than a square inch (6.5 cm²). Researchers retrieved the material after 1,645 sols and inspected it under microscopes, comparing it to control samples that had not been exposed.

The analysis showed minimal visible erosion on the Martian swatches, while lunar regolith samples exhibit deep scratches and surface pitting. Microscopic imaging revealed that Martian particles are rounded, resembling river pebbles, whereas lunar grains are jagged and angular. The smoother morphology is attributed to billions of years of wind-driven transport on Mars, which rounds the particles.

## Why it matters
Abrasion has been a major engineering concern since Apollo, where sharp lunar dust damaged visors, seals, and life‑support filters, and even entered astronauts’ lungs. If Martian dust is indeed less abrasive, suit and habitat designers can prioritize other hazards—such as ultrafine particle inhalation—over heavy‑duty abrasion protection, potentially reducing mass and cost.

Reduced wear also benefits rover and lander hardware, whose optical sensors and moving parts can be fouled by sharp particles. Understanding the true nature of Martian dust informs risk assessments for long‑duration missions, habitat construction, and in‑situ resource utilization.

## How to think about it
Treat Martian dust as a two‑tier risk: its macroscopic smoothness lowers abrasion, but its fine size demands robust filtration and electrostatic mitigation. Design suits with breathable, high‑efficiency filters and consider dust‑repellent coatings for external surfaces. Test materials under simulated Martian dust storms to verify that rounding does not lead to unexpected adhesion or static buildup.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Martian dust is harmless; ultrafine particles can still cause respiratory and mechanical issues if not properly filtered.</Callout>
