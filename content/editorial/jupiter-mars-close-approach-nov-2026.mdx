Jupiter and Mars will share the night sky in a rare close approach on Nov 15 2026, a sight that only happens a few times each decade. The two planets will reach their minimum apparent separation at 9:04 p.m. EST, but they will still be below the horizon for most U.S. observers. By around local midnight the pair will rise together over the eastern horizon, offering a striking visual in the heart of Leo. This alignment is a reminder of the dynamic choreography of our Solar System and a perfect excuse for backyard astronomers to step outside.

## What happened
Jupiter and Mars achieve their closest apparent separation at 9:04 p.m. EST on Nov 15, according to the In‑The‑Sky website. At that moment the planets are still hidden below the western horizon for most of the United States, but they rise together a few hours later, becoming visible just after midnight local time. Their separation is roughly one degree – about the width of a held‑out little finger – and the bright star Regulus sits nearby, marking the head of the Lion constellation.

With a pair of 10×50 binoculars the two planets will share the same field of view, while a modest 4‑inch (or larger) telescope can isolate each body for detailed viewing. The gas giant’s iconic Great Red Spot will rotate into view during the predawn hours of Nov 16, and a light‑green filter can enhance the storm’s contrast, while an orange filter brings out the cloud‑belt detail.

The alignment will gradually dissolve over the following weeks, and the next similarly close pairing won’t occur until July 2029, when the planets meet again in Virgo, about 2.5 years later.

## Why it matters
This event provides a low‑cost, high‑impact outreach opportunity: anyone with a clear sky can witness two of the Solar System’s largest worlds side by side. It reinforces public understanding of planetary motion, orbital periods, and why such conjunctions are infrequent. For educators, the alignment serves as a tangible illustration of orbital resonance and the geometry of heliocentric orbits, linking backyard observation to the legacy of deep‑space missions like Voyager that first visited these giants.

## How to think about it
First, check a reliable sky‑tracker (e.g., Time and Date or a planetarium app) for the exact rise time at your latitude. Aim your eyes or equipment toward the eastern horizon shortly after midnight and locate Regulus as a reference point in Leo. If you have binoculars, center the field on Regulus; Jupiter and Mars will appear on either side within a finger‑width. For telescopic work, start with a low‑power eyepiece to locate each planet, then increase magnification to view details like Jupiter’s cloud belts. Use a light‑green filter for the Great Red Spot and an orange filter for contrast on the belts. Finally, consider a short exposure photograph with a tracking mount to capture both planets in a single frame.

<Callout type="warning">Never point binoculars or a telescope at the Sun; the planets are safe to view only after twilight.</Callout>
