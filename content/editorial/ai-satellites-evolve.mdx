At the opening day of Space Tech Expo Europe, Loft Orbital’s chief technology officer Pieter van Duijn outlined how artificial intelligence is reshaping satellite design and operation. He described a shift from bespoke spacecraft toward modular “satellite‑as‑a‑service” platforms that can be reprogrammed in orbit by AI‑driven software. The conversation highlighted growing industry confidence in AI‑based autonomy, while also flagging data‑security, regulatory, and reliability concerns. If the trend continues, satellite operators could treat space assets like on‑demand vehicles, lowering costs and accelerating new services.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI is turning satellites into programmable, on‑demand assets, but adoption hinges on trust and governance.</Callout>

## What happened
During the first day of the expo, van Duijn explained that operators are increasingly willing to let AI manage routine functions such as attitude control, payload scheduling, and anomaly detection, reducing the need for ground‑segment intervention. He likened the emerging model to renting a car: customers select a satellite “platform” and pay for the specific capabilities they need, while the underlying hardware remains a shared, upgradable asset. The discussion also covered obstacles, including the need for robust validation of AI algorithms, concerns over cybersecurity, and the fact that existing licensing frameworks were written for static hardware rather than adaptive software.

In parallel, larger players such as SpaceX are expanding AI capabilities through dedicated subsidiaries, integrating large language models into telemetry analysis and mission planning. While those efforts are still early, they illustrate a broader industry move toward embedding intelligence directly into the space‑based infrastructure.

## Why it matters
When AI can autonomously adjust a satellite’s behavior, launch providers and users can extract more value from each orbit, shortening development cycles and opening the market to smaller firms that lack deep engineering resources. The rental model lowers upfront capital expenditures, making space more accessible for Earth‑observation, communications, and scientific missions. However, reliance on AI also raises systemic risk: a software flaw could propagate across multiple platforms, and regulators must grapple with liability when decisions are made by opaque algorithms.

## How to think about it
Treat AI‑enabled satellites as a service layer rather than a fixed asset. Evaluate providers on three criteria: the maturity of their autonomous software stack, the transparency of their validation process, and the robustness of their cybersecurity measures. When planning a mission, model cost savings against potential downtime from software updates, and build contingency plans that include manual override capabilities.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on AI for critical safety functions without an independent manual fallback; regulatory compliance must be verified for each autonomous feature.</Callout>
