The James Webb Space Telescope, working with Gemini North, has revealed a compact pair of quasars that existed when the universe was only about a thirteenth of its current age. Designated J1602+4228AB and nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, the duo shines from a time 900 million years after the Big Bang. Their supermassive black holes, weighing roughly 1.5 billion and 400 million solar masses, sit just 18,100 light‑years apart—about one‑fifth the Milky Way’s diameter. This finding upends expectations about how quickly such massive black holes could form.

<Callout type="takeaway">The discovery shows supermassive black holes grew far faster than current theories allow.</Callout>

## What happened
Astronomers using JWST’s infrared capabilities identified the quasar pair through its distinctive spectral signatures, confirming their redshift corresponds to an age of 900 million years post‑Big Bang. Follow‑up observations with Gemini North provided high‑resolution imaging that resolved the two nuclei and measured their separation.

The pair’s black holes have estimated masses of 1.5 billion and 400 million times that of the Sun, derived from the luminosity of their accretion disks and emission‑line widths. Their proximity—just 18,100 light‑years—makes them the most compact quasar duo observed during the Cosmic Dawn era, a period when the first stars and galaxies ignited.

## Why it matters
If such massive black holes existed so early, the mechanisms that feed and grow them must operate on dramatically shorter timescales than current models predict. This challenges the standard picture of black‑hole seed formation and rapid accretion, prompting theorists to revisit scenarios involving direct‑collapse black holes or exotic growth channels. The result also impacts our understanding of early galaxy formation, since quasars influence their host galaxies through powerful feedback.

## How to think about it
Treat this discovery as a boundary condition rather than a definitive rule: it tells us that any viable theory of early black‑hole formation must accommodate objects of billions of solar masses within the first billion years. Compare it with other high‑redshift quasars, explore alternative seed scenarios, and consider the role of dense gas inflows or mergers. Keep an eye on upcoming JWST surveys that will either uncover more examples or confirm this pair’s uniqueness.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate the growth rate of these two black holes to all early quasars without additional observational evidence.</Callout>
