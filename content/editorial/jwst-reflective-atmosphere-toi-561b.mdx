The ultra‑short‑period planet TOI‑561b, a scorching world that circles its star in less than 24 hours, has surprised astronomers with a bright, reflective atmosphere. New JWST/NIRSpec phase‑curve data show the planet’s dayside reflects a large fraction of starlight, contrary to expectations that such close‑in lava worlds should be dark and airless. This finding forces a rethink of how magma‑ocean planets retain or generate atmospheres, and it may reshape the search for secondary atmospheres on other rocky exoplanets.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b’s reflective atmosphere suggests that even the hottest lava worlds can host substantial, possibly secondary, gaseous envelopes.</Callout>

## What happened
JWST observed TOI‑561b over multiple orbital phases, measuring the planet’s thermal emission and reflected light. The phase‑curve analysis revealed a high albedo, indicating that clouds or mineral vapors scatter starlight efficiently. The research team, led by Samuel Boucher, interpreted the signal as evidence for a thin, high‑altitude atmosphere composed of refractory compounds that survive the extreme temperatures.

These results contrast with earlier JWST observations that hinted at volatile gases such as water or carbon dioxide. The new data instead point to a composition dominated by silicate‑based aerosols, a scenario consistent with a magma‑ocean surface outgassing material into a tenuous envelope.

## Why it matters
A reflective atmosphere on a lava world shows that atmospheric loss may be slower or that replenishment mechanisms are active even under intense stellar irradiation. This has implications for planetary evolution models, suggesting that secondary atmospheres can arise quickly on rocky planets. It also expands the types of worlds where astronomers might search for atmospheric signatures, influencing target selection for future missions.

## How to think about it
When evaluating extreme exoplanets, consider three layers: stellar irradiation, surface magma dynamics, and potential atmospheric replenishment. Use phase‑curve albedo as a diagnostic for high‑altitude clouds, but corroborate with spectroscopic data where possible. Treat each detection as a piece of a broader puzzle about how rocky worlds retain or rebuild atmospheres after intense heating.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a high albedo automatically means a stable, long‑lived atmosphere; the observed reflectivity could be transient or driven by episodic outgassing.</Callout>
