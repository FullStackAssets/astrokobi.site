The ultra‑short‑period planet TOI‑561b, once thought to be a barren lava world, has surprised astronomers with a surprisingly bright, reflective atmosphere detected by JWST. Using NIRSpec phase‑curve observations, researchers measured a high albedo that cannot be explained by a bare molten surface alone. This finding suggests that even planets skimming their stars can retain, or quickly regenerate, a secondary atmosphere. If true, it forces a rethink of atmospheric loss and surface‑interior exchange on the hottest rocky worlds.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b hosts a bright, reflective atmosphere despite its searing surface, suggesting secondary atmospheres can survive on ultra‑short‑period lava planets.</Callout>

## What happened
TOI‑561b orbits its G‑type star in less than 11 hours, placing it among the most extreme ultra‑short‑period (USP) planets. Earlier JWST observations hinted at volatile gases, but the new NIRSpec phase‑curve data provide a full‑orbit brightness map, revealing a geometric albedo above 0.5—far higher than expected for a bare magma ocean.

The authors interpret the reflectivity as arising from high‑altitude clouds of silicate vapor or condensates that form as the magma ocean outgasses. Spectral features in the 2–5 µm range are consistent with a thin layer of mineral dust or metal‑oxide particles, pointing to an active exchange between the molten surface and a nascent atmosphere.

## Why it matters
A reflective, possibly secondary atmosphere on a USP planet challenges the prevailing view that intense stellar radiation strips away any volatiles within a few million years. It implies that outgassing from a global magma ocean can replenish an atmosphere faster than stellar winds can erode it. This has cascading implications for models of planetary evolution, the diversity of rocky exoplanet atmospheres, and the criteria we use to assess habitability on close‑in worlds.

## How to think about it
When evaluating extreme rocky exoplanets, consider three linked layers: (1) stellar irradiation that drives surface melting, (2) magma‑ocean outgassing that supplies volatile species, and (3) cloud formation that can boost albedo and shield the surface. A high albedo may signal active surface‑atmosphere exchange rather than a stable, Earth‑like atmosphere. Use JWST phase‑curve data to map brightness variations, then combine with interior models to assess whether observed reflectivity can be sustained over geological timescales.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a bright, reflective atmosphere on a lava world implies surface conditions suitable for life; the clouds are likely composed of hot mineral condensates, not water vapor.</Callout>
