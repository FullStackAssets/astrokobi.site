NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day posted on November 7, 2026 showcases a striking telescopic view of the De Gerlache‑Kocher massif along the Moon’s southern limb. The rugged formation towers roughly 4 km above the surrounding terrain and sits just beyond the lunar south pole, where permanently shadowed craters likely hold water ice. A favorable libration on October 31 gave Earth‑based observers an oblique glimpse of the peaks bathed in near‑continuous sunlight. Scientists see this combination of ice reservoirs and solar power as a sweet spot for the next wave of Artemis crewed landings. The image underscores how lunar geography is shaping humanity’s next foothold beyond Earth.

<Callout type="takeaway">The De Gerlache‑Kocher massif pairs abundant sunlight with hidden ice, making it a prime Artemis landing candidate.</Callout>

## What happened
The APOD image was captured on October 31 when the Moon’s libration angle exposed the south‑polar limb. The photograph reveals the massif’s steep walls and a series of shadowed crater floors that, according to lunar remote‑sensing data, likely contain water‑ice deposits.

Because the peaks receive sunlight for most of the lunar day, they could support solar arrays that generate power for a surface habitat. NASA’s Artemis program has listed the region as a potential site for a crewed landing, citing both resource availability and scientific value.

## Why it matters
The juxtaposition of near‑continuous solar illumination and subsurface ice makes the De Gerlache‑Kocher massif a strategic hub for sustainable lunar operations, influencing mission architecture, in‑situ resource utilization plans, and international competition for a permanent presence on the Moon.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the massif for a base, weigh power generation against terrain risk: map solar‑exposed slopes first, then overlay ice‑rich crater locations. Prioritize modular landers that can hop between safe zones, and design habitats to use the ice for life‑support while shielding against temperature swings. Treat the site as a resource hub rather than a single‑point settlement.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that water‑ice in permanently shadowed craters can be harvested with current technology; extraction methods remain experimental.</Callout>
