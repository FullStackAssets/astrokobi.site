The ESCAPADE mission’s twin probes, Blue and Gold, are set to swing past Earth on November 7 and November 9, 2026. By using Earth’s gravity they will fire a trans‑Mars injection burn that adds more than 1,600 km/h to their velocity. This maneuver shortens the trip to Mars and positions the spacecraft to study how solar charged particles interact with the Red Planet’s magnetic environment. The fly‑bys also offer a rare visual for skywatchers in the western United States and southern Australia. The success of this gravity‑assist sequence will demonstrate the capabilities of the New Glenn heavy‑lift launch system for commercial deep‑space missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">ESCAPADE’s Earth fly‑bys provide a fuel‑saving boost that accelerates the Mars journey and opens new science opportunities.</Callout>

## What happened
ESCAPADE launched on November 13, 2025 aboard Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket from Cape Canaveral. The mission carries two refrigerator‑size probes, nicknamed Blue and Gold, that will first travel outward, loop back toward Earth, and perform a gravity‑assist fly‑by on November 7 (Blue) and November 9 (Gold). At closest approach each probe will fire its engine for a trans‑Mars injection burn, increasing its speed by more than 1,000 miles per hour (≈1,600 km/h).

With the added velocity the probes are slated to arrive at Mars on September 7 and September 9, 2027. Their primary science goal is to measure how charged particles from the Sun interact with Mars’ induced magnetic environment. Observers in the western United States can expect the probes to be brightest between midnight and 2 a.m. PST on the two nights, while southern Australia will see the engine burns around 8:51 p.m. and 9:51 p.m. AEST respectively.

## Why it matters
The gravity‑assist maneuver conserves propellant, allowing a smaller launch mass and reducing overall mission cost—key factors for commercial deep‑space ventures. Faster transit to Mars means the scientific payload can begin observations earlier, improving our understanding of Martian space weather and its impact on atmospheric loss. Successful use of New Glenn for a deep‑space payload also validates the vehicle’s reliability, encouraging future commercial missions beyond Earth orbit.

## How to think about it
If you want to follow the ESCAPADE fly‑bys, start by checking local sunset times and identify a dark, unobstructed horizon facing east. Use a star‑chart app to locate the predicted path and set a timer for the peak brightness windows (midnight‑1 a.m. PST on Nov 7, 1‑2 a.m. PST on Nov 9). A modest binocular or small telescope will make the probes appear as faint moving points against the star field. For deeper appreciation, consider the mission’s engineering trade‑offs: gravity assists reduce launch mass, but they demand precise navigation and timing—principles that will shape many future commercial interplanetary flights.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt to track the probes without proper star‑chart tools; looking directly at bright objects without eye protection can damage your vision.</Callout>
