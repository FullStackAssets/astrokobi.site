The Orionid meteor shower, a debris stream from Halley's Comet, reaches its peak this week. Observers can expect up to 20 meteors per hour after midnight under ideal conditions. A bright waning moon will linger early in the night, dimming the display until it sets. The shower is visible from both Northern and Southern hemispheres, spreading across the sky. Watching it connects us to a centuries‑old celestial visitor.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Orionids offer a bright, fast meteor show this week, best seen after the moon sets.</Callout>

## What happened
The Orionids are produced by particles left behind by Halley's Comet, which passes near Earth every 75 years. This year's peak occurs on Wednesday night into Thursday morning, with the American Meteor Society estimating up to 20 meteors per hour in dark, clear skies. The meteors appear across the whole sky, not just in the Orion constellation, and can leave glowing trains that linger for several seconds.

A bright waning moon will be above the horizon for much of the early night, reducing visibility until it sets late after midnight. Optimal viewing therefore shifts to the hours after moonset, when the sky is darkest. Observers should seek locations far from city lights and with clear, cloudless conditions to maximize their chances.

When fast‑moving space particles enter Earth's atmosphere, they heat up and burn, creating the familiar “shooting stars.” The Orionids are known for their speed and brightness, making them a favorite of amateur astronomers.

## Why it matters
The shower offers a free, spectacular connection to a historic comet that has been observed for millennia, reinforcing public interest in astronomy and space science. Clear viewing conditions can inspire new skywatchers and support citizen‑science projects that track meteor rates. Light‑polluted urban areas miss this natural display, highlighting the growing need for dark‑sky preservation.

## How to think about it
Plan to be outside after the moon sets, typically after 2 a.m. local time, and give your eyes 10–15 minutes to adapt to the darkness. Choose a wide‑open field with minimal light pollution, bring a blanket or reclining chair, and look toward the zenith rather than a specific point. Use a meteor‑watching app to track peak times, but keep your eyes on the sky rather than a screen. Patience is key; meteors arrive sporadically, so stay relaxed and enjoy the quiet night.

<Callout type="warning">Do not use telescopes or binoculars to chase meteors; they are best observed with the naked eye in a dark sky.</Callout>
