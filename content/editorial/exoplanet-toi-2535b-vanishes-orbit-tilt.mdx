The hot‑Jupiter TOI‑2535 b was a reliable beacon for seven years, flashing a regular dip in its star’s light every 8.25 days as seen by NASA’s TESS mission. In early 2026 the expected dip vanished, leaving astronomers puzzled by a planet that seemed to disappear from view. Careful analysis of the full TESS dataset revealed the planet’s orbit was being torqued by an unseen companion, tilting it out of the line of sight. This finding turns a mystery into a vivid example of how planetary systems can dynamically reshape themselves.

<Callout type="takeaway">A hidden neighbor’s gravitational tug can tilt a transiting exoplanet out of view, halting its observable transits for decades.</Callout>

## What happened
During the 2019‑2026 observing window TESS recorded TOI‑2535 b’s transits with a period of roughly eight Earth days, but the timing began to drift by up to six hours early or late. By March 2026 the characteristic dip disappeared entirely, indicating the planet no longer crossed the stellar disk from Earth’s perspective.

Researchers modeled the timing variations and found they were best explained by a massive, non‑transiting companion exerting a strong gravitational pull. The mutual interaction forces both planets into a rapid wobble, tilting TOI‑2535 b’s orbital plane enough that its transit geometry is lost for the next ~30 years, though the hidden neighbor may still transit sooner.

## Why it matters
The case shows that transit surveys can miss planets that temporarily slip out of alignment, biasing occurrence rates for close‑in giants. It also provides a rare dynamical laboratory to study planet‑planet interactions that can reshape orbits on human timescales. Understanding such wobbling systems refines models of planetary formation and migration, especially for hot Jupiters whose histories are still debated.

## How to think about it
When a known transiting planet vanishes, consider orbital inclination changes before assuming the planet is gone. Combine transit timing analysis with radial‑velocity monitoring or direct imaging to hunt for the perturbing body. Treat each system as a dynamic architecture rather than a static clockwork, and factor in possible hidden companions when estimating exoplanet demographics.

<Callout type="warning">Do not conclude that a missing transit means a planet has been destroyed; orbital tilts can render it invisible for decades.</Callout>
