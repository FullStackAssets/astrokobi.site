In April 2026 Earth’s gravity snagged a small near‑Earth object, now designated 2026 QQ8. The asteroid, roughly two meters across, entered a temporary orbit that will keep it near our planet until early 2027. Its capture offers a rare glimpse of how Earth can acquire and lose mini‑moons. Scientists expect the object to swing past at about 353,000 miles before the Sun’s pull releases it. Understanding these fleeting companions refines models of near‑Earth object dynamics and planetary defense.

<Callout type="takeaway">Earth’s capture of 2026 QQ8 shows that temporary mini‑moons are a predictable, short‑lived phenomenon.</Callout>

## What happened
The Minor Planet Center announced the discovery of 2026 QQ8 on 29 October after the object was first observed in August 2026. Orbital calculations indicate it entered Earth’s sphere of influence in April 2026 and will remain gravitationally bound until roughly January 2027.

QQ8 follows a heliocentric orbit of 359 days, almost identical to Earth’s year, and approaches the planet once every ten years. During this encounter its closest approach will be about 353,000 miles (568,000 km), roughly 110,000 miles beyond the Moon’s average distance.

At about two meters in diameter, QQ8 is larger than the average temporary satellite, which modelling suggests is around 0.75 m across and typically stays for nine months. If an object of three meters were captured, simulations predict a stay of 10–20 years.

## Why it matters
Temporary satellites provide natural experiments for testing gravitational capture models, improving predictions of near‑Earth object trajectories, and refining impact risk assessments. Their brief visibility also creates opportunities for radar and optical observations that would otherwise be impossible for objects on solar orbits. While QQ8 poses no impact threat, its study helps calibrate the warning times needed for larger, potentially hazardous asteroids.

## How to think about it
Treat each temporary capture as a case study: first, confirm the object's orbit with radar; second, model its future trajectory to determine escape date; third, integrate the data into broader NEO monitoring systems. Amateur astronomers can contribute by tracking brightness changes, while professional facilities should prioritize high‑resolution imaging during closest approach. The framework turns fleeting events into lasting improvements in planetary‑defense readiness.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a temporary mini‑moon like QQ8 can be harvested or will impact Earth; its atmospheric entry would disintegrate it harmlessly.</Callout>
