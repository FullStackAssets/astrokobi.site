On November 2 2000 the International Space Station welcomed its first long‑term occupants, marking a shift from short shuttle visits to sustained habitation. NASA astronaut Bill Shepherd joined Russian cosmonauts Yuri Gidzenko and Sergei Krikalev aboard the Russian Soyuz TM‑31, the first Soyuz to dock with the ISS. Their four‑and‑a‑half‑month stay included unpacking supplies, installing the station’s first solar arrays, and establishing routines for continuous occupancy. The crew departed on Space Shuttle Discovery, handing the station to Expedition 2. This milestone launched an unbroken human presence in orbit that has now lasted 26 years.

<Callout type="takeaway">Expedition 1 proved the ISS could support crews for months, initiating a continuous human presence in space.</Callout>

## What happened
Expedition 1 consisted of three astronauts—Bill Shepherd, Yuri Gidzenko, and Sergei Krikalev—who launched aboard Soyuz TM‑31 on 31 Oct 2000 and docked with the ISS on 2 Nov 2000. Unlike previous one‑week shuttle missions, this crew was scheduled for a four‑and‑a‑half‑month stay, marking the first long‑duration occupation of the station.

During their tenure the crew unpacked cargo, connected power and data lines, and installed the station’s inaugural solar arrays, dramatically increasing the ISS’s power generation capability. They also performed routine maintenance and scientific experiments, establishing operational procedures for future long‑term crews.

The expedition concluded when Space Shuttle Discovery delivered Expedition 2 and returned the three pioneers to Earth, leaving the ISS fully staffed and ready for continuous occupancy.

## Why it matters
The successful long‑duration stay demonstrated that the ISS could provide life‑support, power, and habitability for months, a prerequisite for deeper space missions and commercial habitats. It cemented international cooperation, showing that a U.S. astronaut could rely on a Russian launch vehicle and vice versa. The uninterrupted human presence that began with Expedition 1 has enabled decades of scientific research, technology development, and inspiration that would be impossible with intermittent flights.

## How to think about it
Treat Expedition 1 as the proof‑of‑concept for permanent orbital habitats. When evaluating future stations—whether lunar, Martian, or commercial—ask whether the core systems (power, life‑support, crew transport) have been demonstrated at the scale of this mission. Use the partnership model as a template for sharing risk and expertise across nations and private firms.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the ISS will remain operational indefinitely; its future decommissioning will require new habitats to inherit its legacy.</Callout>
