The National and Kapodistrian University of Athens recently demonstrated a breakthrough in small‑satellite communications. Its 8U CubeSat, ERMIS‑3, successfully formed a laser link with the Holomondas Optical Ground Station (HolOGS‑1) in Greece. This marks the country’s first optical connection with a low‑Earth‑orbit satellite and validates the precision pointing, acquisition, and tracking (PAT) needed for laser communications. The achievement came only weeks after the satellite’s commissioning, highlighting rapid progress in optical terminal technology. High‑bandwidth laser links could soon replace traditional radio‑frequency channels for data‑heavy missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Optical laser links are now viable for CubeSats, opening a path to much higher data rates from small platforms.</Callout>

## What happened
ERMIS‑3, launched on 30 March 2026 alongside its sister satellites ERMIS‑1 and ERMIS‑2, carried Astrolight’s ATLAS‑1 optical terminal. Using this terminal, the spacecraft acquired and tracked the HolOGS‑1 station, establishing a narrow‑beam laser connection during a brief orbital pass. The link demonstrated the mission’s core objective: precise PAT despite the satellite moving at several kilometres per second.

The ground station, developed by the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki, received the laser signal and confirmed successful data exchange. ESA contributed technical expertise, including environmental testing and optical alignment, to ensure the system could survive the harsh space environment.

## Why it matters
Laser communication offers orders‑of‑magnitude higher data rates than conventional radio links, a critical advantage for Earth‑observation constellations that generate massive volumes of imagery. Demonstrating this capability on an 8U CubeSat proves that even the smallest platforms can support high‑throughput links, potentially reducing the need for larger, more expensive satellites. The success also positions Greece as a new player in the emerging optical‑communications market, encouraging further investment and collaboration across Europe.

## How to think about it
When evaluating whether to adopt laser communications for a CubeSat mission, start by mapping data‑volume requirements against the available ground‑station network. If the mission generates gigabytes of imagery per day, a laser link can dramatically shorten downlink times. Next, assess the orbital geometry: low‑inclination orbits over clear‑sky regions maximize usable windows. Finally, budget for the additional pointing hardware and for a partnership with an optical ground station, recognizing that the upfront cost can be offset by the reduced need for multiple radio‑frequency transceivers.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on laser links in regions with frequent cloud cover; maintain a backup RF channel to ensure mission continuity.</Callout>
