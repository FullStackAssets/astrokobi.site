The international team led by Silvia Onorato announced the discovery of J1602+4228AB, a pair of quasars nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua. The duo shines from a time only 900 million years after the Big Bang, making it the earliest known compact quasar pair. Its separation is so small that the two supermassive black holes appear almost side‑by‑side even at cosmic‑dawn distances. Finding such massive, actively feeding black holes so early challenges our understanding of how they grow.
<Callout type="takeaway">The quasar pair shows supermassive black holes grew far faster than theory predicts.</Callout>
## What happened
Astronomers using the Gemini North telescope on Maunakea and the James Webb Space Telescope identified J1602+4228AB in the Aether survey, confirming it as a close binary quasar system at a redshift corresponding to 900 million years after the Big Bang. The pair’s projected separation is only a few kiloparsecs, making it the most compact quasar duo observed from the cosmic‑dawn era.
The discovery was reported in a paper titled “Nāpōwawā'enakaulua: a close quasar pair at cosmic dawn in the Aether survey” in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. Its rarity underscores how difficult it is to locate and characterize such early‑universe black‑hole binaries.
## Why it matters
Current models of black‑hole growth rely on steady accretion and galaxy mergers to build supermassive black holes over billions of years. A pair this massive and this early suggests either far more efficient feeding mechanisms or unknown physical processes operating in the first billion years. If typical, the finding could reshape timelines for galaxy evolution, star formation, and the re‑ionization history of the universe.

## How to think about it
Treat this discovery as a boundary condition rather than a definitive rule. Incorporate it into simulations as an outlier scenario to explore what extreme accretion rates or merger histories could produce such massive black holes so quickly. Keep an eye on upcoming JWST deep fields and next‑generation surveys, which will either reveal more examples or confirm this pair’s uniqueness, guiding refinements to growth models.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume this single system overturns all black‑hole growth models; more observations are needed before revising the broader theoretical framework.</Callout>
