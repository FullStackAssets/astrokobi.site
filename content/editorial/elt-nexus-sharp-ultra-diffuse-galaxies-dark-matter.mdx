The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is slated for first light in March 2029, and its NEXUS@SHARP instrument promises to open a new window on ultra‑diffuse galaxies (UDGs). These faint, Milky‑sized systems contain far fewer stars than typical galaxies, making them challenging targets for existing spectrographs. By capturing spectra of the globular clusters that orbit UDGs, NEXUS@SHARP can map the surrounding dark‑matter halos. Understanding whether UDGs are dominated by dark matter or are tidal remnants will test key predictions of the Lambda‑CDM model. The breakthrough could reshape theories of galaxy formation and the nature of dark matter.

<Callout type="takeaway">NEXUS@SHARP will let the ELT directly weigh the dark‑matter halos of ultra‑diffuse galaxies.</Callout>

## What happened
The ELT’s first‑light schedule for March 2029 includes the commissioning of NEXUS@SHARP, a multi‑object spectrograph paired with the high‑resolution SHARP spectrograph. A recent paper in New Astronomy outlines how this combination can obtain deep spectra of globular clusters surrounding UDGs, a task beyond the reach of current facilities.

Ultra‑diffuse galaxies are roughly the size of the Milky Way but host orders of magnitude fewer stars, giving them a “puffy” appearance. Their low stellar density makes it difficult to measure their mass profiles, yet globular clusters serve as reliable tracers of the underlying dark‑matter halo.

## Why it matters
If NEXUS@SHARP confirms that UDGs sit in massive dark‑matter halos, it would reinforce the ΛCDM framework and suggest that these galaxies are failed giants. Conversely, finding shallow halos would imply alternative formation pathways, possibly involving tidal stripping or modified gravity, challenging the prevailing cosmological model. The results will influence how astronomers interpret the faint end of the galaxy luminosity function and guide future surveys targeting low‑surface‑brightness objects.

## How to think about it
When evaluating upcoming ELT data, treat each globular‑cluster velocity measurement as a piece of a larger statistical puzzle rather than a definitive halo mass. Combine spectroscopic results with deep imaging to constrain both the spatial distribution of clusters and the stellar light profile. Use Bayesian frameworks that incorporate uncertainties in cluster anisotropy and potential contamination from foreground stars. This approach will yield more robust conclusions about the dark‑matter content of UDGs.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that early ELT observations will immediately resolve the dark‑matter debate; limited sample sizes and systematic uncertainties will require careful analysis and follow‑up studies.</Callout>
