On November 2 2026 NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day highlighted a striking feature over Mars’ volcano Arsia Mons: an unusually long, thin cloud stretching for more than 1500 kilometers. The image, taken in 2018 by ESA’s Mars Express orbiter, shows the cloud during a Martian morning in the spring‑summer season. Scientists call it the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC) and note that it is composed of water‑ice rather than dust. Its size and shape challenge conventional ideas about cloud formation in the thin Martian atmosphere. Understanding AMEC could improve our grasp of both Martian and terrestrial weather systems.

<Callout type="takeaway">The AMEC reveals that massive water‑ice clouds can form on Mars without dust, reshaping our view of alien weather.</Callout>

## What happened
The elongated cloud was first documented by NASA’s Viking Orbiter 2 in 1977, but only recent observations have resolved its structure. Mars Express captured high‑resolution images in 2018 that show a narrow filament extending downstream from the summit of Arsia Mons.

The cloud appears only during the planet’s spring and summer, and only in the early morning when temperatures are low enough for water‑ice to condense. Unlike Earth’s clouds, which often nucleate on dust particles, the thin Martian atmosphere provides too little dust at those altitudes, leading researchers to suspect a different nucleation process.

Wind flowing off the volcano stretches the ice crystals into a tail that can exceed 1500 km, making it one of the longest atmospheric features observed on another world.

## Why it matters
AMEC provides a natural laboratory for studying cloud physics under extreme low‑pressure, low‑temperature conditions, offering clues that can refine climate models for both Mars and Earth. Its existence suggests that water‑ice can aggregate and persist without the dust nuclei typical on Earth, implying alternative pathways for precipitation and atmospheric transport. For future Mars missions, especially those targeting landing sites near volcanic regions, understanding such clouds is essential for assessing visibility, thermal environments, and potential hazards.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Martian atmospheric phenomena, treat each observation as a piece of a larger seasonal puzzle: note the location, time of day, and solar illumination, then compare with model predictions. Incorporate AMEC data into mesoscale simulations to test alternative nucleation pathways, and prioritize repeat observations during subsequent spring‑summer cycles. For mission planners, factor potential cloud cover into entry‑descent‑landing timelines and surface operation schedules.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the cloud’s behavior is representative of the entire Martian atmosphere without additional seasonal data.</Callout>
