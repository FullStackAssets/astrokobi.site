The world is seeing heat waves and crop failures with alarming frequency, and a new Indian startup is stepping in with a bold idea: a "thermometer for Earth" in space. SatLeo Labs plans to launch 15 microsatellites that will orbit the planet in low Earth orbit by early 2030, delivering sub‑10‑meter thermal data in near real‑time. The project, called Pyro, uses onboard artificial intelligence to interpret the heat signatures it captures. If successful, the constellation could give city planners, farmers and emergency responders instant, fine‑grained temperature maps.

<Callout type="takeaway">SatLeo Labs’ 15‑satellite constellation will provide sub‑10‑meter thermal maps of Earth in near real‑time, offering new tools for heat‑island mitigation, crop monitoring and wildfire detection.</Callout>

## What happened
SatLeo Labs first tested its technology with TAPAS‑1, a thermal access platform launched on October 1 aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 as part of the Transporter‑18 rideshare mission. The demonstrator proved that the company’s custom hardware can survive the space environment and that its sensors can capture surface temperatures with a resolution finer than 10 meters (33 feet). Building on that success, the team is now preparing the Pyro constellation of 15 microsatellites, each equipped with thermal sensors and AI processors, slated to complete its deployment by 2030 if funding and launch schedules remain on track.

The satellites will measure heat radiating from Earth’s surface and, thanks to their high resolution, can identify temperature variations at street level. This granularity enables the detection of urban heat islands, early signs of crop water stress, forest fire ignition points, industrial emission hotspots and even sudden temperature spikes from missile launches.

## Why it matters
Having near real‑time, high‑resolution thermal data would transform how cities manage heat stress, allowing planners to design cooler streets and schedule maintenance during cooler periods. Farmers could receive alerts weeks before visible crop damage, improving irrigation decisions and reducing water waste. Early wildfire detection could save lives and property, while the defense community gains a new sensor for missile launch monitoring. The commercial potential is also significant: navigation apps could incorporate live heat maps to help gig‑economy workers avoid the hottest routes.

## How to think about it
When evaluating projects like Pyro, consider the balance between technological ambition and practical deployment. First, assess the maturity of the hardware: a successful demonstrator is a good sign, but scaling to 15 satellites introduces new risks. Second, examine the data pipeline: onboard AI can reduce latency, but ground‑station bandwidth and processing capacity must keep pace. Third, think about the ecosystem: who will buy the data, how will it be integrated into existing decision‑making tools, and what regulatory hurdles might arise. Finally, keep an eye on cost curves; as microsatellite manufacturing matures, launch costs may fall, making such constellations more viable.

<Callout type="warning">The project faces several uncertainties: launch schedules for the remaining satellites have not been finalized, sensor calibration in orbit remains unverified at scale, and regulatory approval for commercial thermal imaging data could delay deployment.</Callout>
