A stunning selfie of Earth floated into the news feed this week, captured by Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk during a routine EVA. On Oct. 29, 2026, Kutryk and NASA astronaut Anil Menon stepped outside the International Space Station at an altitude of 267 mi (430 km) to install a new roll‑out solar array. While tethered to the station, Kutryk turned his helmet camera toward the planet, producing a clear view of the South Atlantic below. The image highlights both the human side of orbital maintenance and the technical upgrades that keep the ISS powered. It also marks the first Canadian spacewalk since 2019, underscoring Canada’s growing role in deep‑space operations.

<Callout type="takeaway">Astronauts can turn routine maintenance into iconic moments, but every EVA remains a high‑risk operation.</Callout>

## What happened
During the pre‑Halloween EVA, Kutryk and Menon began work on the eighth and final roll‑out solar array, a modular panel that unfurls to increase the station’s power generation. The new array is expected to boost the ISS’s available power by roughly 30%, extending the life of onboard experiments and supporting upcoming commercial modules.

The spacewalk also served as a symbolic moment for Canada: it was the first Canadian EVA since 2019, and the selfie captured the planet’s curvature against the blackness of space, reminding viewers of Earth’s fragility.

NASA officials noted that the eight‑array suite will be the last major power‑generation upgrade before the station’s planned retirement in the early 2030s.

## Why it matters
The photograph bridges public fascination with the gritty reality of orbital maintenance, turning a technical task into a visual narrative that can inspire future engineers and policymakers. The solar‑array upgrade directly impacts the ISS’s ability to host long‑duration research, which feeds into plans for lunar habitats and deep‑space missions. International collaboration is also highlighted, as a Canadian astronaut works side‑by‑side with a NASA partner, reinforcing the multinational nature of the station.

## How to think about it
Treat spacewalk selfies as a communication tool, not a mission objective. When evaluating such events, weigh the outreach value against the operational risk budget of the EVA. Use the image to spark discussions about power needs for future habitats, but keep safety protocols front‑and‑center. For educators, the photo can illustrate orbital mechanics, Earth observation, and international partnership in a single frame.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that taking personal photos during EVAs is routine; safety protocols prioritize mission tasks over non‑essential activities.</Callout>
