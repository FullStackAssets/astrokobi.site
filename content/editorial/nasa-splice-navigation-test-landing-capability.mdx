On Aug 27, NASA’s Armstrong Flight Research Center saw an Alta‑X drone equipped with the Safe and Precise Landing – Integrated Capabilities Evolution (SPLICE) payload complete a simulated lunar descent and landing at Rogers Dry Lake. The SPLICE system generated high‑resolution digital elevation maps from lidar data and autonomously steered the drone away from unsafe terrain. This flight marked the first end‑to‑end demonstration of the guidance, navigation, and control suite that could be used on future robotic and crewed landers. By proving the technology in a realistic environment, NASA moves the hardware one step closer to operational status for upcoming Moon Base and Mars missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">SPLICE proved autonomous terrain‑aware landing can work on the Moon and beyond.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA integrated the SPLICE payload, developed at Johnson Space Center, onto the Alta‑X drone and flew it from Rogers Dry Lake. During the flight the payload mapped the terrain, identified a hazardous patch, and redirected the drone to a safer spot, demonstrating real‑time decision making. The test also validated communications, telemetry, and command‑control links between the drone and the payload.

The Armstrong team confirmed that data handling and software interfaces performed as expected, paving the way for a full‑scale flight at Voyager Technologies in Mojave later this month. NASA is now preparing to make the SPLICE software available in its catalog for commercial lunar‑lander partners.

## Why it matters
Autonomous navigation reduces reliance on ground‑based guidance, which is limited by signal latency on distant bodies. A system that can assess landing safety in‑situ enables higher‑precision touchdowns, opening the door to more complex surface missions and larger payloads. Both NASA’s Artemis lunar initiatives and future Mars exploration concepts stand to benefit from this capability, and commercial partners can leverage it to lower risk and cost.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a landing system, weigh the trade‑off between autonomy and verification. Consider SPLICE as a modular capability that can be added to existing guidance stacks, but plan for extensive ground‑testing before crewed deployment. For mission planners, treat autonomous terrain assessment as a risk‑reduction layer rather than a complete replacement for pre‑mission site surveys.

<Callout type="warning">Do not schedule crewed lunar or Martian landings using SPLICE until it reaches a higher technology readiness level and completes an operational flight demonstration.</Callout>
